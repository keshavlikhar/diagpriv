\section{Privacy--Utility Analysis}
\label{sec:privacy}
The measurement surface is exactly the released \texttt{ModelView}:
question, structured rows, log messages and attributes, evidence IDs,
source inventory, and ordering. The local evaluator compares each annotated
raw scalar with what remains visible after transformation; it does not use
hidden generator metadata as an exposed value. Analysis version
\texttt{privacy-exposure-analysis-v1} produces a vector rather than a weighted
privacy score. An empty eligible denominator is reported as
\texttt{not\_applicable}, not as zero exposure.

The vector separates (i) exact annotated literal retention,
(ii) preservation of equal pairs and separation of unequal pairs,
(iii) repeated-entity frequency histograms, (iv) cross-source equal and
unequal joins, (v) original length/character-class/separator shape,
(vi) source record counts, sensitive-field presence, and distinct-entity
counts, (vii) original absolute timestamp retention, (viii) parseable
relative order and exact inter-event differences, and (ix) classified
secret fail-closed status. An ordinary short-string literal metric uses a
six-character embedding cutoff to limit accidental substring matches;
classified secrets use strict verbatim scanning with no length cutoff.
These dimensions cannot be averaged into a defensible single privacy number.

Table~\ref{tab:scalar-exposure} is generated directly from the frozen
development exposure artifact, not transcribed from a model result.
All four scalar transformations remove every annotated direct literal in
these selected Incident 001 and 002 views. This is narrower than saying
every identifying fact is hidden. Redaction also loses all measured
cross-source equal joins and collapses many distinct values; its surviving
equal pairs are not faithful linkage because unequal-pair separation falls.
HMAC, tokenization, and format-preserving pseudonymization retain 276/326
Incident 001 cross-source equal pairs, not 326/326. Fifty payment-ID joins
are intentionally lost through independent path namespaces, while essential
request-ID linkage remains. In Incident 002 those three methods retain
248/248 measured reference joins. Format-preserving pseudonyms retain
476/476 and 190/190 original shape patterns; opaque HMAC and tokenization
retain 0 on that specific shape measure. Thus similar literal-removal
counts can coexist with quite different linkage or format exposure.

Frequency and cardinality are equally distinct from literal identity.
Incident 001 eligible frequency histograms are 20/20 raw, 0/20 redacted,
and 15/20 under each deterministic pseudonym condition; Incident 002 has
6/6 raw and pseudonymous versus 0/6 redacted. Record counts and annotated
field presence remain intact in every scalar condition (Incident 001:
20/20 source counts and 476/476 fields; Incident 002: 30/30 and
190/190). Furthermore, these methods leave unannotated absolute
timestamps visible: 139/139 in Incident 001 and 478/478 in Incident 002.
The transformed view may therefore still convey operational timing and
membership cues.

Raw retains 204/204 original absolute timestamp occurrences and all
5,004 measured order and exact-gap relations. Relative-time removes
the original absolute literals (0/204) while preserving all measured
order and exact millisecond-gap relations. Ablation leaves no parseable
temporal pair (0/5,004 evaluable), so its order and gap zeros mean
\emph{unavailable}, not observed reversed chronology. Both temporal
conditions still expose all 192/192 annotated non-temporal operational
identifier literals. Relative offsets may reveal absolute times to an
observer with an external time anchor.

These results characterize the released representation, not adversarial
success. They do not estimate re-identification probability, membership
inference, auxiliary-data attack success, provider retention, compliance,
legal de-identification, differential privacy, or $k$-anonymity. A
controlled synthetic HMAC fixture shows that identical values under the
same key and namespace can link across incidents; the corpus does not
estimate a cross-incident attack success rate. No current development
annotation is classified as secret, so the secret denominator is
\texttt{not\_applicable}, not a claim of zero risk. Synthetic tests exercise
the fail-closed policy.

Utility and exposure must be interpreted side by side. Incident 002's
opaque pseudonyms retain selected joins but erase the intended structural
clue; Incident 003's relative-time view drops calendar identity while
retaining the temporal relations required by the paired oracle. Neither
case supplies a generally optimal transformation rule. Instead, the
matched examples illustrate why the diagnostic dependency must be named
before evaluating what a release should preserve.
